**Title:** Why Do We Shiver When We're Cold? #shorts

**Description:**

>
> Facts checked against kid-friendly science sources.
>
> #shorts #kidsscience #learnwithkids #shivering #humanbody

**Tags:** kids science, science for kids, fun facts for kids, shivering, humanbody

**Sources checked:**

- The Children's Museum of Indianapolis, 'Why do I shiver when I'm cold?': cold air takes away body heat; tiny sensors in your skin send messages to your brain that you need to warm up; the brain tells muscles to move; muscles quickly tighten and loosen over and over, which is shivering; the energy gives off heat that helps keep you warmer
- Live Science, 'Why do we shiver when we're cold?' (citing Oregon Health & Science University): skeletal muscles tense and relax in speedy bursts, and jaw muscles twitching make your teeth chatter
- Old Farmer's Almanac: moving muscles generates heat, which is why we get warm when we exercise; shivering is a sign we should get in from the cold

## 33. Why Is the Ocean Salty?

**File:** 33-salty-ocean.mp4 · **Publish:** 26 Nov 2026, 17:00 IST · **Thumbnail text:** IT'S ROCKS!

**Title:** Why Is the Ocean Salty? #shorts

**Description:**

> The ocean's salt starts in rocks on land! Rain wears the rocks down, rivers carry the salt to the sea, and if you spread it all over the land it would be taller than a 40-story building.
>
> Facts checked against kid-friendly science sources.
>
> #shorts #kidsscience #learnwithkids #ocean #earthscience

**Tags:** kids science, science for kids, fun facts for kids, ocean, earthscience

**Sources checked:**

- NOAA National Ocean Service, 'Why is the ocean salty?': salt comes from runoff from land and openings in the seafloor; rocks on land are the major source; rain is slightly acidic and wears down rocks, releasing ions that streams and rivers carry to the ocean; seawater seeping into seafloor cracks is heated by magma and released through vents
- NOAA National Ocean Service, 'Why is the ocean salty, but rivers flowing into it are not?': rain replenishes freshwater in rivers so they don't taste salty; the ocean collects salt and minerals from all the rivers that flow into it
- USGS Water Science School, 'Why is the Ocean Salty?': if the salt in the ocean were spread evenly over Earth's land it would form a layer more than 500 feet (166 m) thick, about the height of a 40-story office building

## 35. How Do Bees Make Honey?

**File:** 35-bees-honey.mp4 · **Publish:** 27 Nov 2026, 17:00 IST · **Thumbnail text:** 1/12 TEASPOON

**Title:** How Do Bees Make Honey? #shorts

**Description:**

> Bees sip flower nectar, pass it from bee to bee, and fan it with their wings until it turns into thick, sticky honey. One bee makes only a tiny drop in its whole life: it takes 12 bees to make one teaspoon!
>
> Facts checked against kid-friendly science sources.
>
> #shorts #kidsscience #learnwithkids #honey #animals

**Tags:** kids science, science for kids, fun facts for kids, honey, animals

**Sources checked:**

- Wonderopolis, 'How Do Bees Make Honey?': worker bees use long straw-like tongues to suck nectar, similar to sugar water, from flowers; they store it in a separate stomach called a crop; back at the hive they pass it to other worker bees who break it down further until it is deposited into a honeycomb cell; nectar is about 80% water so bees fan it with their wings to evaporate it until it becomes thick honey; bees seal the cells with beeswax; a single worker bee makes only about one-twelfth of a teaspoon of honey in its lifetime
- Indiana DNR Kids, 'Honeybees': an average worker honeybee makes about 1/12th of a teaspoon of honey in her lifetime; bees evaporate nectar by fanning their wings, then cap the honey
